Today we liberate trigonometry from the physical confines of right-angled triangles. In a right triangle, an angle can never equal or exceed $90^\circ$. By placing a triangle inside a coordinate circle of radius 1, we can evaluate trigonometric functions for any angle imaginable—whether positive, negative, or greater than a full rotation. We will master the spatial structure of the unit circle, quadrant transformations, and exact radical ratios.

---

## 🧭 The Core Strategy Blueprint

### 👥 Parent Analytics Summary
* **The Core Gap:** Students frequently struggle to memorize the unit circle as a massive table of arbitrary values, leading to inevitable signs errors (e.g., confusing $\cos(\frac{2\pi}{3}) = \frac{1}{2}$ with $-\frac{1}{2}$). They treat each quadrant as an entirely new set of rules instead of seeing them as simple geometric reflections of a single reference triangle.
* **The Target Objective:** We anchor the unit circle as a dynamic coordinate engine where $\cos(\theta) = x$ and $\sin(\theta) = y$. Your student will learn to map any target angle back to its acute first-quadrant **reference angle** ($\theta_k$), determine signs instantly using coordinate quadrants (the CAST engine), and use the special triangles from Week 2 to find exact radical values.

### 🎒 First-Principles Script (The Feynman Technique)
> **Read this aloud to lock in the structural intuition:**
>
> "Let's build the unit circle from scratch so you never have to memorize a table of values again. Imagine drawing a perfect circle on a coordinate grid, centered at the origin $(0,0)$, with a radius of exactly **1 unit**. 
> 
> Let's choose a point $P(x,y)$ on the circle's edge and draw a line from the center out to that point. This line forms an angle $\theta$ with the positive $x$-axis. If we drop a vertical line straight down from $P$ to the $x$-axis, we create a right-angled triangle inside the circle where the hypotenuse is exactly 1.
> 
> 
>
> Let's look at our basic right-angle trigonometric ratios for this internal triangle:
> * $\cos(\theta) = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} = \frac{x}{1} = x$
> * $\sin(\theta) = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} = \frac{y}{1} = y$
> * $\tan(\theta) = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Adjacent}} = \frac{y}{x} = \frac{\sin(\theta)}{\cos(\theta)}$
> 
> This is a massive conceptual leap! On the unit circle, **cosine is simply the $x$-coordinate** of the point, and **sine is simply the $y$-coordinate**. 
> 
> Because coordinates can be negative, sine and cosine can now be negative! 
> * In **Quadrant I** (top-right), both $x$ and $y$ are positive, so **All** functions are positive.
> * In **Quadrant II** (top-left), $x$ is negative but $y$ is positive, so only **Sine** is positive.
> * In **Quadrant III** (bottom-left), both $x$ and $y$ are negative, making their fraction positive, so only **Tangent** is positive.
> * In **Quadrant IV** (bottom-right), $x$ is positive but $y$ is negative, so only **Cosine** is positive.
> 
> To find the exact value of an angle in any quadrant, you only need to look at its vertical reflection in Quadrant I. This acute baseline angle is called the **reference angle**."

### 🧪 Tier 4: Junior Paper 3 Investigation (Problem 10)
*Focus: Periodic coordinate coordinate proofs, parametric identities, and universal variable eliminations.*

#### 10. Problem 10: The Parametric Coordinate Circle Identity
Let us execute a rigorous proof exploring how coordinates behave along the boundary edge of any scaled circle. Consider a point $P(x,y)$ that coordinates a path around a circle centered at origin $(0,0)$ with a scaled radius length of exactly $R$. The position vector lines an angle $\theta$ relative to the standard horizontal positive axis across any arbitrary domain.

Suppose we define two completely separate parametric tracking equations, $A$ and $B$, that monitor the coordinates of point $P$ under variable scaling conditions, such that:

$$A = R\cos(\theta) + R\sin(\theta) \quad \text{and} \quad B = R\cos(\theta) - R\sin(\theta)$$

* **Part A:** Express the coordinate variables $x$ and $y$ directly as functions of parameters $R$ and $\theta$ using basic right-angle circular trigonometry.
* **Part B:** Calculate the expanded polynomial expressions for $A^2$ and $B^2$ completely. Do not attempt to simplify any trigonometric blocks yet.
* **Part C:** **Prove that** if we combine these squared tracking metrics through addition, the angular parameter $\theta$ eliminates from the engine entirely, leaving an invariant geometric relationship that depends only on the system's radius scale:

$$A^2 + B^2 = 2R^2$$

<details>
<summary>💡 View Strategy Hint</summary>

**Structural Connection:** This advanced challenge bridges **Week 6 unit circle coordinate definitions** with **Week 1 literal binomial expansion loops**.

For Part A, use the circular definitions $x = R\cos(\theta)$ and $y = R\sin(\theta)$. For Part B, use the standard perfect square expansion rule $(u \pm v)^2 = u^2 \pm 2uv + v^2$. For Part C, add your two expanded expressions together. Look closely at the middle cross-product terms—they will cancel out completely! Then, look for a way to factor out $R^2$ and apply the core identity $x^2 + y^2 = R^2$ (or $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$).
</details>

<details>
<summary>📋 Expand Full Mark Scheme</summary>

* **Step 1 [Part A]:** State the structural coordinate definitions for a circle of radius $R$: **$x = R\cos(\theta)$** and **$y = R\sin(\theta)$** **[1A]**
* **Step 2 [Part B]:** Expand the first squared expression using binomial laws:
  $$A^2 = (R\cos(\theta) + R\sin(\theta))^2 = R^2\cos^2(\theta) + 2R^2\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta) + R^2\sin^2(\theta)$$ **[1M]**
* **Step 3 [Part B]:** Expand the second squared expression using binomial laws:
  $$B^2 = (R\cos(\theta) - R\sin(\theta))^2 = R^2\cos^2(\theta) - 2R^2\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta) + R^2\sin^2(\theta)$$ **[1M]**
* **Step 4 [Part C]:** Set up the addition tracking line for $A^2 + B^2$:
  $$A^2 + B^2 = \left[R^2\cos^2(\theta) + 2R^2\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta) + R^2\sin^2(\theta)\right] + \left[R^2\cos^2(\theta) - 2R^2\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta) + R^2\sin^2(\theta)\right]$$ **[1M]**
* **Step 5 [Part C]:** Cancel the internal cross-product terms vertically: The $+2R^2\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta)$ and $-2R^2\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta)$ balance out to zero. **[1M]**
* **Step 6 [Part C]:** Combine the remaining like terms together:
  $$A^2 + B^2 = 2R^2\cos^2(\theta) + 2R^2\sin^2(\theta)$$ **[1A]**
* **Step 7 [Part C]:** Factor out the common constant coefficient variable block:
  $$A^2 + B^2 = 2R^2\left[\cos^2(\theta) + \sin^2(\theta)\right]$$ **[1M]**
* **Step 8 [Part C]:** Apply the fundamental Pythagorean identity ($\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$) to complete the proof:
  $$A^2 + B^2 = 2R^2(1) = 2R^2$$
  Because the trigonometric component collapses to 1, the angle variable $\theta$ is completely eliminated, verifying the structural invariant relationship. **[1A]** *(Identity verified completely—Q.E.D.)*
</details>
