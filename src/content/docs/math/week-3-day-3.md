## 🧭 The Core Strategy Blueprint

### 👥 Parent Analytics Summary
* **The Core Gap:** Most high school students treat the midpoint and gradient formulas as disconnected, plug-and-play recipes. When IB DP examiners present a Paper 3 problem asking them to trace a moving point that maintains an equal distance between a curve and a fixed node, visual intuition collapses. 
* **The Target Objective:** We are training students to see gradients not just as "rise over run," but as structural direction parameters. By linking midpoints with negative reciprocal gradients, we establish the algebraic foundation for deriving the equations of **geometric loci** (the paths traced out by points moving under specific geometric constraints).

### 🎒 First-Principles Script (The Feynman Technique)
> **Read this aloud to lock in the structural intuition:**
>
> "Let's strip coordinate geometry down to its absolute first principles. 
>
> A **midpoint** isn't a magical formula; it is quite literally just the exact arithmetic average of your positions. If you stand at $x = 2$ and I stand at $x = 8$, the middle ground is simply $\frac{2 + 8}{2 = 5}$. We apply that exact same averaging logic to both the $x$ and $y$ coordinate axes simultaneously to lock down a central balancing anchor.
>
> A **gradient ($m$)** measures the structural rate of change along a trajectory. When we say two paths are perfectly perpendicular, we mean their directions are decoupled by exactly a $90^\circ$ rotation. Mechanically, this means if one line climbs at a rate of $\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$, its perpendicular twin must fall at the inverted rate of $-\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta y}$. This is why their product must always equal $-1$: 
> 
> $$m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$$
>
> A **geometric locus** is simply a mathematical crime scene: it is the complete set of all possible coordinate points left behind by a moving dot that is forced to follow a strict geometric rule. For example, if a dot is forced to stay exactly the same distance from two fixed anchors, the locus it traces out is a perfect perpendicular bisector. If you master the midpoint and the negative reciprocal gradient, you can map out any geometric path an examiner throws at you."

#### 10. Problem 10: The Perpendicular Bisector Envelope Locus
Let us define a fixed reference anchor node point located at $A(0, 2)$. Suppose a variable point moves along the flat $x$-axis line tracking positions at $P(\theta, 0)$, where $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$ acts as a dynamic positioning parameter.

* **Part A:** Find the exact coordinates of the midpoint $M$ tracking segment $AP$ in terms of variable parameter $\theta$.
* **Part B:** Derive the general algebraic equation of the perpendicular bisector line of segment $AP$ expressed as a functional layout model $y = f(x)$ containing parameter $\theta$.
* **Part C:** Let us investigate the intersection point $Q$ where two independent perpendicular bisectors—generated by two distinct position settings $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$—cross each other. **Prove** through algebraic expansion that as $\theta_2 \longrightarrow \theta_1$, the intersection point coordinates transform cleanly into a limiting locus curve defined by the quadratic function $y = \frac{1}{4}x^2 + 1$.

<details>
<summary>💡 View Strategy Hint</summary>

**Structural Connection:** This problem models an advanced Paper 3 investigation that bridges **Week 2 perpendicular systems** with early non-linear locus optimization modeling. 

For Part C, set up a simultaneous system using two equations ($y_{\theta_1}$ and $y_{\theta_2}$), isolate the $x$-coordinate intersection by clearing variables, and take the limiting parameter track as they converge.
</details>

<details>
<summary>📋 Expand Full Mark Scheme</summary>

* **Step 1 [Part A]:** Average components between $A(0,2)$ and $P(\theta,0)$: **$M = \left(\frac{\theta}{2}, 1\right)$** **[1A]**
* **Step 2 [Part B]:** Calculate segment trajectory gradient: $m_{AP} = \frac{0 - 2}{\theta - 0} = -\frac{2}{\theta}$ **[1M]**
* **Step 3 [Part B]:** Invert slope parameter to lock down perpendicular trajectory: $m_{\perp} = \frac{\theta}{2}$ **[1M]**
* **Step 4 [Part B]:** Form line equation tracking through your midpoint coordinate $M$:
  $$y - 1 = \frac{\theta}{2}\left(x - \frac{\theta}{2}\right) \implies \mathbf{y = \frac{\theta}{2}x - \frac{\theta^2}{4} + 1}$$ **[1A]**
* **Step 5 [Part C]:** Set up simultaneous equation balance lines for two distinct parameters $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$:
  $$\frac{\theta_1}{2}x - \frac{\theta_1^2}{4} + 1 = \frac{\theta_2}{2}x - \frac{\theta_2^2}{4} + 1$$ **[1M]**
* **Step 6 [Part C]:** Transpose terms and factor out matching variable segments to isolate $x$:
  $$\frac{\theta_1 - \theta_2}{2}x = \frac{\theta_1^2 - \theta_2^2}{4} \implies \frac{\theta_1 - \theta_2}{2}x = \frac{(\theta_1 - \theta_2)(\theta_1 + \theta_2)}{4}$$ **[1M]**
* **Step 7 [Part C]:** Divide across by common difference factor $(\theta_1 - \theta_2)$ and scale to solve for horizontal coordinate:
  $$x = \frac{2(\theta_1 + \theta_2)}{4} = \frac{\theta_1 + \theta_2}{2}$$ **[1M]**
* **Step 8 [Part C]:** Evaluate the system limit as parameter boundaries converge ($\theta_2 \longrightarrow \theta_1$):
  $$x \longrightarrow \frac{\theta_1 + \theta_1}{2} = \theta_1 \implies \mathbf{\theta_1 = x}$$ **[1M]**
* **Step 9 [Part C]:** Substitute your parameter equivalence statement $\theta = x$ directly back into your Part B general locus model formula equation:
  $$y = \frac{x}{2}(x) - \frac{x^2}{4} + 1 = \frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^2}{4} + 1 = \mathbf{\frac{1}{4}x^2 + 1}$$ **[1A]** *(Locus proof verified—Q.E.D.)*
</details>
